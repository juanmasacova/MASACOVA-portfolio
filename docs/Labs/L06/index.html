L6 – Snap Fit Speaker Case

Objective

This week I had to parametrically design a small part that snap fits onto a feature of an artifact we measured in class. The point was to use tolerances, allowances, and the fit classes from lecture (RC, LC, LT, LN, and FN) to size the mating features, then print the part and test the fit. My artifact is a small oval speaker, and my part is a case that holds it in place with four snap fit lips.

Design Idea

The case is a hollowed out rectangular box. The speaker drops into the open end, and four small lips at the opening catch the speaker's front flange so it can't fall back out. I'm not using the speaker's four mounting holes, so nothing in the design touches them.

  • Internal cavity: matches the speaker's width and length, plus an allowance on each side.
  • Walls: 1.6 mm thick.
  • Lips (x4): each one protrudes 1 mm. The side the speaker enters from has a 45° chamfer so the flange can push past it. The underside is flat, so once the speaker is snapped in, the flat face sits on top of the flange and holds it. The lips stay away from the electronics on the back of the speaker.
  • Cable hole: a half-round cutout at the bottom of the case, like a mouse hole, with a 5 mm radius. I cut off the JST style plug, so the wires are bare and pass through the hole easily.

Research: Tolerances, Allowances, and Fits

Definitions

Machinery's Handbook (31st Ed., p. 640) defines tolerance as the amount of variation permitted on the dimensions or surfaces of machine parts. It defines allowance as a prescribed difference in dimensions used to get different classes of fits between parts. So tolerance is how much a single dimension is allowed to wander, and allowance is the gap (or overlap) I choose on purpose between two mating parts.

Which Fit Class Is This?

The speaker sits in the case like a locational clearance fit (LC). The parts have to go together freely by hand, but they should still locate each other without a lot of play. The lips are a different story. During insertion the flange has to push past a 1 mm lip, which is a temporary interference, and after that the lip springs back and holds the speaker. So the overall behavior is LC for the pocket, with the snap fit lips supplying the retention. It is not an LN or FN fit, because nothing is pressed together permanently.

What the Handbook Says About My Sizes

Table 6 (ANSI/ASME B4.1-1967 (R2020), Machinery's Handbook 31st Ed., p. 650) gives standard tolerances in thousandths of an inch, by nominal size range and tolerance grade. I converted my measurements to inches (1 in = 25.4 mm) and pulled the row for each one.

The precise grades a locational fit is built on are down around 0.02 to 0.06 mm for these sizes. My printer can't hold anything close to that. Table 7 in the handbook relates machining processes to tolerance grades, and 3D printing isn't on it. The closest processes it lists are milling and drilling, which run roughly from grade 10 to 13. The Bambu Lab A1 holds about 0.2 mm on small features (my working assumption, which the test print ended up confirming), and that lines up with grade 12 in the table: 0.20 mm on the width and 0.25 mm on the length.

Allowance Decision

I set the clearance to 0.2 mm per side. That puts the internal cavity at the speaker size plus 0.4 mm in each direction, so the speaker slides in by hand, and it stays inside the grade 12 tolerance band I expect from the printer. It is still a lot bigger than what the handbook expects for an LC fit, but the handbook numbers assume machining, so I used them as the design intent and used the printer's real accuracy to pick the actual number.

Why These Parameters

The three speaker dimensions are the ones I measured, so if I swap in the other speaker or remeasure, the case follows. The clearance is a parameter on its own because it was the number I was least sure about, and I wanted to be able to change it in one place after the test print. Wall thickness and lip protrusion are kept as parameters because they control how stiff the case is and how hard the speaker is to push in.

Did the Values Change?

  • Clearance: I started with 0.2 mm per side, briefly looked at 0.4 mm per side to be safe with the printer, then went back to 0.2 mm. At 0.4 mm the allowance is well past what the handbook table expects for a locational fit, and it would have been harder to justify. 0.2 mm keeps the design closer to the table and it matches grade 12 for the printer.
  • Lip relief cuts: I first planned cuts above and below each lip so the wall could flex more easily. I left them out of the final model. TO ADD: why you decided they weren't needed.

Lip Design

The lip is 1 mm tall in every case. The entry side is a 45° chamfer so the speaker's flange can ride up and over it, and the other side is flat so the lip can't slide off once it's on top of the flange. I didn't do any beam calculations for this assignment. The lip's job is only to hold a light speaker in place.

CAD Process

3D Printing

Printer and Layout

I printed on a Bambu Lab A1 with a 0.4 mm nozzle. I printed two cases at once, side by side on the plate, one for each of the two speakers. Each case is about 32 mm × 43.5 mm in footprint, so the pair takes up a small part of the A1's 256 mm build plate. TO ADD: your reasons for the layout (why side by side, why centered).

The print took 28 minutes and used 13 g of filament for both cases, according to the printer's completion screen. TO ADD: filament type.

Build Orientation and Supports

The back of the case sits flat on the plate and the cavity faces up. That is the easiest way to print it, it keeps the walls strong since the layers stack up along the length of the wall, and it needs no supports. The lips are only 1 mm overhangs, and the 45° chamfer on the entry side keeps them printable without support. Because there are no supports, there was nothing to size, and nothing to remove afterward.

Lessons Learned

Handbook tolerances are for machining: The tables in Machinery's Handbook assume machining, and the tolerances for an LC fit are far tighter than what an FDM printer can hold. I used the table as the design intent and picked the actual allowance from the printer's real accuracy.

Keep the allowance adjustable: Making the clearance a parameter meant I could change it in one place. I went from 0.2 mm to 0.4 mm and back to 0.2 mm without rebuilding anything.
