L9 – Laser Cutting: Parametric Lamp Diffuser

Objective

This week's goal was to design, laser cut, assemble, and document a parametric construction kit that accounts for laser kerf. I worked with a partner, and we each document the project in our own portfolio. Our kit is a lamp diffuser: a shade cut from a 3 mm wood sheet that slides together with press-fit tabs and slots and sits on a 3D printed base that holds the light source. The shade has to spread the light that travels upward from the base and diffuse it gently outward. The kit has 3 unique laser cut part types (a big ring, a small ring, and a column) and 8 laser cut pieces in total (2 big rings, 2 small rings, and 4 columns).

The engineering anchor for the week is Genichi Taguchi's robust design. The joints should not just fit once. They should fit across the whole sheet, even though the kerf, the thickness of the wood, the focus, and the position on the bed all vary. So the work is organized the way the lab asks: measure the material and the kerf, run a fit test, choose the clearance, and then cut the final kit.

Design Decisions

  • Shape. Four rings stacked around a central light source, with the two wide rings in the middle, give the shade a rounded profile that spreads the light gently outward. The open gaps between the columns let the light through, and the rings keep the shade rigid. The columns are curved to follow that profile.
  • Joints. The column tabs press into slots in the small rings, which is the press fit the lab requires, and the big rings sit in notches on the columns. I used the same kinds of slot throughout so that one measured value, the kerf, controls every joint.
  • Control factors (the things we chose). The slot width is the material thickness plus the clearance, and the slot length is a fixed 3/8 in (9.525 mm) adjusted for the kerf. The rings are sized so that two sets fit on one 12 × 12 in sheet, and the inner ring sits inside the opening of the outer ring so that the circles share material.
  • Noise factors (the things we cannot fully control). Thickness variation in the wood, kerf variation, focus, and where a part sits on the laser bed. The focus error is absorbed by the kerf and the clearance, and it is hard to put a number on because it changes with the thickness of the wood and the flatness of the bed.
  • Tight fits first, then adjusted. The wood is fairly compliant, so the first cut used a minimal clearance of 0.005 mm, only covering the variation in the stock thickness (about ±0.2 mm on a 3 mm sheet). After the first cut we saw how the parts actually fit and changed the slot values through the equations to get a better fitting part.

Engineered Allowances

Material thickness. I measured the sheet with calipers in 3 places and used the mean, because a sheet labeled 3 mm rarely measures 3 mm.

By the time we cut the final parts we were using a different wood, which measured between 2.70 and 2.80 mm. I set PlateThickness to 2.75 mm, the middle of that range. Because the model is parametric, the change was one number.

Kerf. I measured the kerf with a square coupon, D × D, cut on the laser cutter. I measured the square with calipers in the X and Y directions and calculated the kerf as D minus the measured width, three times in each direction.

Kerf measurement #123Mean
X direction0.11 mm0.10 mm0.12 mm0.11 mm
Y direction0.17 mm0.21 mm0.19 mm0.19 mm

The kerf in the Y direction is about 0.08 mm larger than in the X direction. I used the average of the two, 0.15 mm, as the kerf variable.

Slot and tab dimensions. The laser removes a strip of material, the kerf, along every path. A slot drawn at the plate thickness would therefore cut out wider than the plate, and the joint would be loose. So each slot is drawn narrower than the plate by the kerf, and the clearance is added on top. For the first cut I kept the clearance at 0.005 mm, because the wood is soft and compliant and a tight fit seats without cracking it. That gave a column notch of 2.60 mm (2.75 − 0.15), a ring slot width of 2.605 mm (2.75 + 0.005 − 0.15), and a ring slot length of 9.38 mm (3/8 in minus the kerf plus the clearance).

After the first cut we saw how the parts fit, and we changed the values through the parametric equations to get a better fitting part. The slots in the final cut files are larger: the ring slot is 2.85 mm wide by 9.37 mm long, and the column notch is 3.50 mm wide. The 2.85 mm slot width is the 2.75 mm plate plus 0.25 mm of clearance, less the 0.15 mm kerf, so the final clearance is about 0.25 mm.

Live Demo Answers

  • What kerf did we measure, and how? 0.15 mm, the mean of X (0.11 mm) and Y (0.19 mm). We cut a D × D square coupon, measured it with calipers in both directions, and calculated the kerf as D minus the measured width.
  • What clearance did we choose, and why? We started at 0.005 mm, because the kerf already accounts for the material the laser removes and the wood is compliant. After the first cut we saw how the parts fit and opened the slots through the equations. The final ring slot is drawn at 2.85 mm, which is about 0.25 mm of clearance over the 2.75 mm plate after the kerf, and the final column notch is 3.50 mm.
  • One variable you can change, and what updates. PlateThickness. With the first-cut equations, changing it from 2.75 mm to 2.88 mm changes the column notch width from 2.60 mm to 2.73 mm and the ring slot width from 2.605 mm to 2.735 mm, and the extrusion depth of every part follows it.

Manufacturing, including Process Research

Process Research: Laser Cutting

A laser cutter focuses a high-energy beam onto a small spot on the workpiece, and the spot melts, burns, or vaporizes the material. The laser delivers so much power in such a small area that the rest of the part stays cool while the material under the beam is removed. The beam comes together at a point and then spreads out again, so the walls of a cut are very slightly tapered. That is more noticeable on thick material, and the height of the laser above the workpiece can change it. The material that the beam removes is the kerf, which is why it has to be measured and designed around. The two common types are CO2 and fiber lasers. The machine we used is a CO2 laser, whose long wavelength is absorbed well by organic materials such as wood and by plastics, but is reflected by metals. Fiber lasers have a shorter wavelength that metals absorb, so they are the choice for cutting metal. They also focus to a much smaller spot, which puts more energy into each unit of area, so they cut faster and hold tighter tolerances.

Laser cutting is a viable manufacturing process because it is fast and repeatable and needs no tooling for each part, only the blank sheet and the energy. Compared with FDM printing:

  • Speed. A laser cuts a flat part in minutes. The job that cut our 8 shade pieces had a run time of 3 minutes 34 seconds. The 3D printed base parts, which are smaller, took about 1 hour 54 minutes to print. The advantage gets bigger as the parts get larger.
  • Geometric limits. A laser cutter makes only 2D parts, with small exceptions for etching, while an FDM printer can make 3D shapes. In return, the laser cut edge is nearly perpendicular to the sheet, so inside corners and slots are crisp, and a part can be made from one flat sheet. This is the reason the shade is built from flat parts that slot together, and the base, which needs a 3D shape, is printed.
  • Cost. Both processes need a machine, but the laser cutter's cost per part is mostly the sheet and the energy, and there is no tooling to buy for a new shape.
  • Material options. The laser we used cuts wood, plastics, and paper but not metals. An FDM printer is limited to thermoplastics, and a fiber laser opens up the metals.

Kerf Coupon and Fit Test

Before cutting the final kit we ran the two tests that the lab requires. The kerf coupon is a D × D square. We measured its width in X and Y, and the results are the kerf table in the Design section: 0.11 mm in X and 0.19 mm in Y. The fit test was a small parameter study in the style of Taguchi. It checks the press fit between a tab and its slot, built from the measured thickness and kerf. When we cut the parts we saw how they fit, and we used the parametric equations to change the slot values and get a better fitting part. The values before and after the change are in the Parameter Table.

Layout and Nesting

Cut path on the 12 × 12 in sheet. I made these choices for the layout:

  • The two ring sets sit on the diagonal of the sheet, which is the arrangement that lets two 7 in rings fit on a 12 in sheet.
  • Each small ring is nested inside the opening of a big ring, because the small ring is cut out of the material that the big ring's opening would waste. Both parts are drawn on the same 127 mm circle.
  • The four columns go in the space left around the two circles, in alternating orientations, so the curved outlines fill the corners of the sheet instead of leaving it unused.
  • All the inside features, the slots and the openings, are cut before the outer profiles, which is the inside-out vector sorting that the software uses. That keeps each part held in the sheet until its slots are cut.
  • The origin for both the sketch and the material is the upper left corner.

Air Assist and Exhaust

Air assist and exhaust were both on for the whole cut. The air assist blows the smoke and debris out of the cut, and the exhaust removes the smoke from the machine.

Edge Quality

The wood was thin enough that we could not see any taper from the laser focus on the cut edges. The edges had some soot, but it was minimal.

Mistakes, Troubleshooting, and Changes

  • The wood changed between the test and the final cut. I measured the first sheet at 2.88 mm, but the wood we used for the final parts measured between 2.70 and 2.80 mm. Since the slots are driven by PlateThickness, I could change one variable, set it to 2.75 mm, and let every slot regenerate.
  • The first slot sizes were too tight. The first cut used a clearance of only 0.005 mm. After we saw how the parts fit, we changed the values through the parametric equations: the ring slot went from 2.605 mm to 2.85 mm wide and the column notch from 2.60 mm to 3.50 mm. Because the slots are driven by variables, this was an edit to the values, not a redraw.
  • The kerf was not the same in both directions. The X and Y measurements differ by 0.08 mm. The test variable table carried a kerf of 0.004 in (0.10 mm), and the final model uses the measured average of 0.15 mm.
  • The test geometry was not the final geometry. The test uprights had a different, angular shape, and the final columns are curved with notches. The slot math carried over, but the final column shape was a new design.

Reflection

1. Where did my measured values differ from what I assumed, and what would have happened if I had used the assumed values? The wood was never exactly 3 mm. The first sheet measured 2.88 mm and the sheet we cut measured about 2.75 mm. The kerf also differed from the 0.10 mm in my test variable table, at 0.15 mm on average. If I had used the nominal 3 mm and ignored the kerf, I would have drawn the slot at 3.00 mm, and it would have cut out at about 3.15 mm. That is 0.40 mm wider than the 2.75 mm plate, about 15% of its thickness, and the tabs would wobble. Ignoring only the kerf, a slot drawn at 2.75 mm would have cut out at about 2.90 mm, loose by about 0.15 mm without any clearance added.

2. Using Taguchi's quality loss function, describe one joint that works but is not on target. The four slots in a small ring run in two orientations. Two have their narrow side along X, where the kerf is 0.11 mm, and two have it along Y, where the kerf is 0.19 mm. I designed all four for the average kerf of 0.15 mm, so the two slots along X cut out about 0.04 mm narrower than the target and the two along Y about 0.04 mm wider. All of them fit, but none is exactly on target. Taguchi's loss grows with the square of the distance from the target, so a deviation of 0.04 mm costs 4 times as much as one of 0.02 mm. If the part were made by the thousands, that would show up as a population of lamps where some joints are tight enough to need force and some are loose enough to wobble.

3. A real product that uses cut sheet parts joined by tabs and slots. Flat pack furniture is one. If the panels came out thicker than the slots, the customer would have to force the panels together and could crack a panel or a slot. If the panels came out thinner, or the kerf was bigger than planned, the joints would be loose and the finished piece would wobble.

4. If I had one more cut, what would I change? I would orient the slots in the small rings so that every slot has its narrow side along the same direction on the bed. All four slots would then get the same kerf, and I could use the kerf measured in that direction instead of the average of X and Y, which would put every slot on target.
