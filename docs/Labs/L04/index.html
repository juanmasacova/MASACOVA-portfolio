L4 – Tolerance Fit Checker

9/10/2026

Objective

This week's job was to design my own benchmark part to figure out where the Prusa Core One actually hits its limits, then compare what I found to the FDM row of the class Design Rules for 3D Printing chart. The only catch was that the print couldn't go over an hour, since everybody is fighting for time on the machines.

Parameter

There were four options: overhang angle, pull strength, tolerance gauge, and dimension calibration. I went with the tolerance gauge. Basically the question is, how close does the printed part come to what I drew in CAD?

This one felt the most useful to me. A part can look totally fine and still be off by just enough that it won't go into the thing it's supposed to fit in. If I ever design two pieces that slide or snap together, I need to know how much room to leave between them, and guessing isn't really a plan.

Research: What the Design Rules Say

Before designing anything I checked the FDM row of the Design Rules for 3D Printing chart. Two boxes matter for this test:

  • Tolerance: ±0.3%, with a lower limit of ±0.3 mm. On something as small as a 10 mm peg, 0.3% is only 0.03 mm, which is way under the lower limit. So the number that actually applies to my part is ±0.3 mm.
  • Connecting/moving parts: 0.5 mm of clearance between two parts that fit together.

Remember that 0.5 mm. It comes back later.

Prediction

Before printing, I figured the 9.96 mm peg would be the first one to fit. My thinking was that the nozzle on the Core One is 0.4 mm, so I just assumed the dimensional tolerance would be on that same kind of scale, and a peg 0.04 mm under the hole would be enough. Honestly I thought the whole thing was going to fit a lot better than it did.

Design

I went with a peg and socket gauge, split into two parts:

  • The peg plate: a flat base with five square pegs sticking up out of it. Every peg has equal sides, and each one is 0.02 mm smaller than the one before it: 10.00, 9.98, 9.96, 9.94, and 9.92 mm. I cut each peg's size into the plate right in front of it so I'd know which was which.
  • The socket: a separate piece with a 10 × 10 mm square hole, the exact size of the biggest peg.

The idea is pretty simple. Try every peg in the socket, and the first one that goes in tells you how much clearance the printer really needs. The 10 mm peg is exactly the size of the hole, so it's there as the "no way this fits" baseline.

Print

I printed this on Thursday, September 10th on Prusa Core One #2 with my lab partner James McAdam. The print took about 40 minutes, so it made it under the one hour limit with room to spare.

Mistake: the numbers I cut into the plate were way too small. They technically printed, but they came out rough and don't look good at all. Looking at the chart afterwards, engraved details on FDM are supposed to be at least 0.6 mm wide and 2 mm high, so that's something I should've checked before slicing.

Results

I measured everything with calipers. They were reading in inches, so I converted it all to millimeters (1 in = 25.4 mm) to keep everything metric. The "room in socket" column is the measured socket hole (10.06 mm) minus the measured peg.

Only the 9.92 mm peg went in, and it went in so tight that I can't get it back out. It's pretty much a press fit now. So the smallest designed gap that worked was 0.08 mm total, which is 0.04 mm per side, and even that was too tight for anything that's supposed to come apart.

So why didn't 9.96 and 9.94 fit?

This part confused me at first. The first two pegs make sense, one is bigger than the hole and the other is the same size. But on paper the 9.96 peg had 0.05 mm of room and the 9.94 peg had 0.13 mm, and neither of them would go in.

My best guess is the corners. Looking closely, there's some bulging at the corners of the pegs and inside the socket. When the nozzle goes around a sharp corner it has to slow down and change direction, and a little extra plastic gets pushed out right there. Calipers measure flat side to flat side, so they completely miss those bumps. The peg corners bulge out and the socket corners bulge in, so the two squares end up hitting each other at the corners even when the flat sides have plenty of room.

Lessons Learned

Was it different from what I thought? Yep. I guessed 9.96 would fit and it wasn't even close. It took going all the way down to 9.92 before anything went in, and that one barely did.

How does it compare to the design rules? For dimensional accuracy the Core One actually did fine. The worst peg was off by 0.11 mm, which is well inside the ±0.3 mm the chart gives for FDM. If you only look at percentages, three of the pegs were over 0.3%, but that's exactly why the chart has the ±0.3 mm lower limit, since percentages get tiny on small parts. So for accuracy, the printer met the spec.

Where I fell short was clearance. The chart says 0.5 mm between connecting parts, and my biggest designed gap was 0.08 mm. I basically built my whole test inside the range the chart already said wouldn't work, and my results agreed with the chart.

Things I'd change next time:

  1. Way bigger clearance range. My gaps went from 0 to 0.08 mm. Next time I'd go from something like 0.1 mm up to 0.5 mm, so I can actually find where it switches from press fit to slip fit instead of only catching the super tight end.
  2. Bigger steps between pegs. My calipers read to 0.001 in, which is about 0.025 mm, and my pegs only changed by 0.02 mm each. So the differences between pegs were right at the edge of what I could even measure, which is probably why the measured sizes don't step down evenly.
  3. Get rid of the sharp corners. Square pegs are extra sensitive to corner bulging. Round pegs, or squares with small fillets or chamfers on the vertical edges, would take the corners out of the picture so I'm testing the actual fit and not the corner bumps.
  4. Bigger numbers. Check any engraved text against the 0.6 mm wide and 2 mm high minimum from the chart so the labels actually come out readable.
  5. Nozzle size isn't tolerance. My prediction was based on the 0.4 mm nozzle, but the nozzle diameter really just sets how wide each line is. It doesn't tell you how close the printer lands to a dimension. Stuff like corner bulging and how the plastic squishes out matters more.
  6. Plan for getting the part back out. My 9.92 peg is now stuck in the socket for good. A little tab to grab, or a hole in the bottom of the socket to push it out, would've saved it.

The print itself took about 40 minutes, and the whole lab took about 3 hours start to finish.
