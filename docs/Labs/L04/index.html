L4 – Tolerance Gauge Test

9/10/2026

Objective

Find out what the Prusa CORE One can actually hold for a dimension, instead of assuming. Design a gauge, print it, measure it, and then hold that number up against the FDM row of the class design rules chart to see how the real printer compares to the documented spec.

Parameter

There were four benchmark tests to pick from and I went with the tolerance gauge test. It checks how close a printed dimension lands to the number you typed into CAD, and past that, how much clearance two parts need before they will go together at all.

That second half is the part I cared about. A part can look completely fine sitting on the bed and still be dimensionally wrong, and you usually do not find out until you try to assemble it and it will not go.

Prediction

Before I sliced anything I wrote down that 9.96 mm would be the first peg to drop into the 10.00 mm socket. My logic was that the nozzle is 0.4 mm, so I figured the printer's dimensional accuracy was somewhere around that scale, and 0.04 mm of undersize would leave plenty of room.

I was expecting this thing to do better than it did. That is not how it went.

Design

The artifact is a two piece peg and socket gauge. Simple on purpose.

  • Socket: one block with a single square pocket at a nominal 10.00 mm × 10.00 mm. This is the reference and it never changes.
  • Peg plate: one plate with five square pegs sticking up off it, at 10.00, 9.98, 9.96, 9.94, and 9.92 mm on a side. All of them square, so both sides always match.
  • Labels: the size of each peg cut into the plate right in front of it, so I could tell which one I was holding.

I stepped the pegs by 0.02 mm, which spreads five data points across a 0.08 mm range. I picked that because I expected the answer to land in the first step or two, and anything coarser would have jumped straight over it. Spoiler, that step size turned out to be a problem for a totally different reason, which I get into in the lessons.

I went with squares instead of round pins on purpose. A square peg has four flat faces and it can only go in four ways, so if anything is off it binds and you know right away. A round pin can spin to whatever angle fits best and hide a small error, which would have made the printer look better than it is. Turns out picking squares mattered a lot more than I thought it would.

The Part That Did Not Add Up

Here is where it got interesting. I subtracted each peg from the socket to see how much room there should have been.

The first two make sense. The 10.00 peg is physically fatter than the hole and the 9.98 is exactly the same size as the hole, so neither one was ever going in.

But the 9.96 and the 9.94 both measured smaller than the socket, one of them by 0.127 mm, and they still would not go. By the caliper those two should have dropped right in. So whatever was stopping them was not showing up in my measurement.

It was the corners

When I looked at the pegs closely there is visible bulging at the four corners. The flats are fine, the corners are fat. And a caliper laid across two flat faces will never see that, because it is measuring the skinniest part of the peg and completely ignoring the part that is actually doing the binding.

A square peg in a square hole jams at the corners before it jams anywhere else, so the corners are the only dimension that really mattered here and they are the one dimension I did not measure.

There are two things stacking up:

  1. The peg corners bulge outward. Where the nozzle turns a 90 degree corner it slows down and the plastic piles up a little, so the corner ends up with more material on it than the straight sections.
  2. The socket corners are rounded inward. This one is just physics. A 0.4 mm round nozzle cannot cut a sharp inside corner. The best it can do is leave a radius of about 0.2 mm in each corner of that pocket, so the pocket is not really a square, it is a square with four rounded corners.

That second one is worth doing the math on. If a square pocket has a 0.2 mm radius in each corner, a peg with sharp corners has to shrink by about 0.117 mm across the flats before its corners clear that radius. That number lands right between my 9.94 peg, which had 0.127 mm of clearance and did not fit, and my 9.92 peg, which had 0.152 mm and barely did.

So the corner geometry pretty much accounts for the entire gap between what the caliper predicted and what actually happened. The printer was not as far off as the fit test made it look. My part geometry was just fighting the process.

Comparison to the FDM Design Rules

The Protolabs chart lists FDM tolerance as ±0.3% with a lower limit of ±0.3 mm. On a 10 mm feature, 0.3% is only 0.03 mm, which is under the 0.3 mm floor, so the number that actually applies to my part is ±0.3 mm.

My worst measured deviation was 0.109 mm. That is about a third of the documented limit, so on the number that governs, the CORE One beat the spec and it was not close.

Run it as a percentage and it flips. 0.109 mm on a 10 mm feature is 1.09%, which is more than three times the 0.3% figure. The chart says take whichever of the two is larger, so the printer still passes, but it is worth noticing that the percentage half of that spec is written for parts way bigger than a 10 mm cube. At this size the absolute floor is doing all the work and the percentage is basically meaningless.

The other row that matters here is connecting / moving parts, which lists 0.5 mm of recommended clearance for FDM. My tightest actual clearance was 0.152 mm and that one is welded in place permanently. So I never even got into the range the chart recommends, and the part that did assemble is exactly the failure the 0.5 mm number exists to prevent. If I had designed a real assembly off my own gauge result instead of off the chart, I would have built something that goes together one time and never comes apart.

Lessons Learned

1. Nozzle diameter is not a tolerance number. This is the one that killed my prediction. I assumed 0.4 mm of nozzle meant something about accuracy. It does not. Nozzle diameter is a resolution number, it tells you the thinnest line the machine can put down. It says nothing about how close the finished dimension lands to what you drew. Mixing those two up is how you design a press fit you cannot assemble, which is more or less what I did.

2. Measure the dimension that actually controls the fit. I measured across the flats because that is the obvious thing to put a caliper on. But a square peg binds at the corners, so the flats were never the controlling dimension. That is why my numbers said two pegs should fit and reality said no. Next time I measure corner to corner across the diagonal, or I put pins in the socket corners, or I just do not use a sharp square peg in the first place.

3. Square peg in a square hole is a bad fit geometry for FDM, and there is a standard fix. Machinists ran into this on end mills a long time ago and solved it with corner relief, little extra cuts at each inside corner so a sharp corner has somewhere to go. Same idea works here. Either put a chamfer or radius on the peg corners so they stop fighting the pocket radius, or cut relief into the socket corners. If I redid this I would relieve the corners and the whole test would be measuring the printer instead of measuring corner geometry.

4. My caliper could not resolve my own design steps. It reads to 0.001 in, which is 0.0254 mm. I designed the pegs 0.02 mm apart. So my increments were smaller than one tick on the tool I was checking them with, which means I could not have told two neighboring pegs apart even if they printed perfectly. I should have flipped the caliper into mm, and I should have used a step size my instrument could actually see, something like 0.05 mm.

5. The error is not a constant offset, so you cannot just fudge it in CAD. I commanded a 0.08 mm spread across the five pegs and got a 0.203 mm spread back, about two and a half times what I asked for. If the printer were just running fat by a fixed amount, every deviation would be the same number and it is not, it walks from +0.109 down to -0.014. That says the slicer is quantizing something, probably shuffling perimeter widths as the target size crosses a threshold. Which means you cannot fix this by subtracting one magic number in CAD, you have to test at the size you care about.

6. My labels were too small and the chart already told me so. The size numbers I cut into the base came out mushy and hard to read. The design rules chart lists FDM engraved detail at 0.6 mm wide and 2 mm high and mine were under that. Annoying part is I had the chart the entire time and still eyeballed the text size. Next time I pull the feature size straight off the chart before cutting anything, and I move the labels onto the top face where they are not competing with the perimeter walls for room.

What I would change: relieve the corners, go to 0.05 mm steps, start the sweep around 9.90 instead of 10.00 since a 10.00 peg in a 10.00 socket was never going to work, measure everything in mm before doing any fit testing, and size the labels off the chart.

Actual time: about 40 minutes of print time, roughly 3 hours for the whole lab start to finish.
