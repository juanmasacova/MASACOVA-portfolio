L7 – Ornithopter Linkage

Objective

This week's goal was to design, 3D print, and document a working linkage or mechanism that performs a defined motion. I could use purchased hardware such as screws, bolts, and pins, but every other functional part had to be my own design and 3D printed. I designed a hand-cranked ornithopter: turning a wheel makes two wing arms rise and fall together, like a bird's wings flapping.

Design

Purpose

The mechanism turns a steady rotation of a wheel into a flapping motion of two wings. I chose it because I study aerospace, and while learning about the history of the industry I found ornithopters very interesting. Before fixed-wing aircraft, many early designers tried to copy how birds fly, and I wanted to build a working version of that idea. It does not fly. It is a desk model that I turn by hand, so I can show how the linkage moves.

How It Works

  • Wheel (crank): a hole in the wheel, off its center, carries a bolt. As the wheel turns, that bolt travels in a circle.
  • Vertical link (connecting rod): connects the offset bolt on the wheel to the center pin, so the center pin moves up and down as the wheel turns.
  • Wing links (levers): each wing link pivots on a fixed bolt in the base, about one quarter of the way along its length. The inner end has a short slot where the center pin sits. When the center pin moves up and down a small amount, the long outer end of each wing link swings through a much larger arc. The long outboard side is about three times the length of the short inboard side, so the wingtip moves about three times as far as the pin.
  • Why a slot: the inner ends of the two wing links meet at the center pin, but each one swings on its own arc around its own pivot. If both were simply pinned together at the center, the mechanism would lock. The slot lets the center pin slide a few millimeters along each wing link as it swings.

The wheel is the only input. Turning it once makes the wings flap down and back up once.

Final assembly (SolidWorks): the base stands on two legs. The wheel is at the lower center, the vertical link runs up from the wheel to the center of the wing links, and the two wing links form the long horizontal bar across the top.

Layers

The parts are stacked in four layers, each 5 mm thick, so the whole assembly is 20 mm thick (the legs are not counted). From back to front:

  1. Layer 1: the base plate.
  2. Layer 2: the wheel, the first wing link, and one 5 mm spacer (which lifts the second wing link's pivot).
  3. Layer 3: the second wing link, and the other 5 mm spacer (between the wheel and the vertical link).
  4. Layer 4: the vertical link.

Splitting the wing links across two layers lets their inner ends cross at the center without hitting each other.

Design Decisions

1. How the two wing links meet at the center.

  • Alternative A: pin both wing links directly together at the center. I ruled this out because the inner ends travel on two different arcs, so the mechanism would lock.
  • Alternative B: add a short extra link between the vertical link and each wing link. This works, but it adds parts and joints.
  • Chosen: a slot in each wing link. It needs no extra parts, and the pin only has to slide a few millimeters along the slot.

2. Where the wing pivot is.

  • Alternative: put the pivot near the middle of each wing link, so the inboard and outboard sides are about equal. The wing would need a much bigger up-and-down push from the wheel to flap the same amount.
  • Chosen: a pivot about one quarter of the way along the wing link. The long outboard side is about three times the inboard side, so a small up-and-down motion of the center pin gives a large flap at the tip and the wheel only has to move the pin a short distance.

3. Stacking the parts in layers with spacers.

  • Alternative A: put every part in one plane. The two wing links would have to cross at the center, and they would collide.
  • Alternative B: use longer spacers or add more layers. That makes the mechanism thicker and needs more bolt length.
  • Chosen: four 5 mm layers, with two printed 5 mm spacers on the crank pin and on one wing pivot. The total thickness is 20 mm, and every joint can use the same M3 bolt, 20 mm long.

3D Print

Printer and Material

I printed on a Bambu Lab A1 with a 0.4 mm nozzle and a textured build plate, in Overture Matte PLA, using the 0.20mm Standard @BBL A1 process in Bambu Studio.

Printer, nozzle, filament, and process, with the Advanced settings toggle switched on.

Parts on the Plate

I printed all of the parts on one plate: the base, two legs, the wheel, the vertical link, two wing links, and two spacers. The slicer arranged them automatically, and I left that layout.

Every part lies flat on the plate, so the bolt holes point straight up and print as round as possible.

Slicer Settings

Layer height and line width: layer height and initial layer height are both 0.2 mm. Line widths are 0.42 mm for the default, outer wall, top surface, internal solid infill, and support, 0.45 mm for the inner wall and sparse infill, and 0.5 mm for the initial layer.

Walls and shells: 2 wall loops, 5 top shell layers (1 mm), and 3 bottom shell layers.

Infill: 15 percent, grid pattern.

Speed: initial layer 50 mm/s and initial layer infill 105 mm/s. Outer wall 200 mm/s, inner wall 300 mm/s, sparse infill 270 mm/s, internal solid infill 250 mm/s, and top surface 200 mm/s.

Supports: turned off. All of the parts lie flat, so I did not need supports.

The Two Required Slicer Changes

Two slicer defaults often ruin printed joints, so I changed both. The first one is elephant foot compensation.

Elephant foot compensation: the first layer is pressed into the bed and spreads out wider than the model. That makes holes near the bed come out undersized and makes pins flare at the base, which is a problem for bolts turning in holes. In Bambu Studio the setting is under Process, Quality, Precision.

Precision settings: I changed elephant foot compensation from the default of 0.075 mm to 0.15 mm, which is highlighted as changed. The setting shrinks the first layer's outline inward by that amount on each side, so doubling the value should make the first layer's holes come out about 0.15 mm larger in diameter than they would with the default. I left X-Y hole compensation and X-Y contour compensation at 0, and auto circle contour-hole compensation turned off, so that the only first-layer change is this one.

TO FILL: the difference I saw. Compare a hole printed at 0.075 mm against one printed at 0.15 mm, for example by measuring the bed-side and top-side diameters of the same hole with calipers.

Seam position: every perimeter starts and ends at a seam, which leaves a small bump or line. A seam on a bolt hole or on the hub where parts touch can make the joint bind in one spot every time it turns. In Bambu Studio the setting is under Process, Quality, Seam.
