L5 – Snap Fit Quick Release Buckle

Objective

This week I had to design and print a snap fit assembly: two parts that hold together using elastic deformation. The flexure gets sized with the beam deflection equation, and the whole thing gets built parametrically in CAD.

Design Idea

I'm making a quick release buckle, the kind with two flexing prongs that you squeeze together to let go. I picked it because I'd actually use it, and I'd rather not print stuff that ends up in a drawer. The buckle has two parts: the male part with two cantilever prongs that each end in a hook lip, and the female part (the receptacle) with a window for each lip to catch on.

Research: Material

I'm using Overture Matte PLA. The manufacturer's technical data sheet (TDS v5.1, July 2024) lists these values for prints in the X-Y direction:

PropertyValue
Young's modulus, E (X-Y)1879 MPa
Tensile strength (X-Y)20.9 MPa
Tensile strength (Z, across layers)10.9 MPa
Flexural modulus (X-Y)2671 MPa
Elongation at break (X-Y)29.2 %

The TDS doesn't list a yield strength, so I'm using the 20.9 MPa tensile strength as the limit. That's the conservative choice since yield can't be higher than the strength at break. I'm using the tensile modulus (1879 MPa) and not the flexural one, since the tensile test is the one that goes with the tensile strength I'm comparing against. I looked for a second independent source for matte PLA but everything I found just repeats Overture's own numbers, so this is the one source. Matte PLA is also weaker than regular PLA, so the numbers here are lower than the ones you'd see for a general PLA.

The Z direction number is the one to remember. Across layers the material is about half as strong (10.9 vs 20.9 MPa). That's why the flexure has to be printed with the bending inside the layers and not across them.

Assumptions: each prong gets the transverse load P at its tip on its own, and each prong carries the full axial load F, even though two prongs would really share it. That's the conservative way to do it. Conversion used: 1 lbf = 4.448 N, and MPa = N/mm² so I can keep everything in N and mm.

Choosing the Width and Base

The stress limit alone decides how short the prong can be, and the load only decides how wide it has to be (worked out on the handwritten pages below). That is what killed my first tries:

  • 3 lbf: even a 12 × 8 mm section was over the allowable stress. It doesn't work at any reasonable size.
  • 1 lbf: worked, but the prong had to be either 65 mm long (hollow box) or 16 mm wide (at 50 mm long).
  • 0.5 lbf: gets the width down to a normal 8 to 10 mm. That's what I went with.

Width b is the width of one prong, measured out of the bed plane (the print height in Z since I'm printing flat). Base h is the thickness in the bending direction. I chose b = 8 mm and h = 4 mm, with a beam length L = 50.6 mm measured from the root to the tip. The 5 mm lip is included in that, so the straight part of the beam is 45.6 mm.

Box beam note: the lab hint says to use a box beam because infill doesn't add much support. With 5 walls at 0.4 mm on each side I get 2 mm of solid wall per side, and at h = 4 mm the two walls meet in the middle. So the section is full plastic with no hollow, and I treated it as a solid rectangle.

Iteration: the first pass used 3 lbf and it failed, so I lowered the load to 1 lbf and then to 0.5 lbf, and lowered the pull from 7 lbf to 5 lbf. Along the way I dropped the 65 mm hollow box and the 50 mm, 16 mm wide prong. I settled on an 8 mm wide prong that is 50 mm long including the lip.

Parametric Design

Male Part: Prong Assembly

Global variables driving the male part geometry.

Sketch on the top plane. I sketched an arbitrarily sized buckle base to grab onto and attach a band, sized to the gap plus two times the prong thickness (33 mm total). This base also anchors the prongs. I added the rough shape of the prongs with their lips and dimensioned everything parametrically using the global variables. I also added a center prong that doesn't grab onto anything but contacts the top surface of the female part. I made it T-shaped to maximize surface area and add strength in compression. Everything is fully constrained, parametrically where possible.

Exited the sketch, selected it in the feature tree, and extruded it to the prong width using the global variable.

Added a 1 mm fillet to all internal corners, except the lip's bottom corner, since a fillet there would make it easier for the part to slide back off.

Added a 0.5 mm fillet to the outside bottom corners of the lip. The lip is only 1 mm tall, so a 1 mm radius would cover from the outside to the inside of it. Printing a straight sharp corner isn't a good idea either, and I learned in L04 that 0.5 mm is about the minimum tolerance the printer can hold, so that set the fillet size.

Added 1.5 mm radius fillets to the remaining outside corners. These help the print quality and the look of the part, and don't touch any functional area.

Finished male part.

Female Part: Receptacle

Global variables driving the female part geometry.

Sketch on the top plane. Sketched a rectangle and sized its length and width using the BoxThickness and BoxLength variables.

Exited the sketch, selected it in the feature tree, and extruded it to the BoxWidth variable.

Used the shell feature, clicking on the side where the buckle enters, with the Wall variable as the shell thickness.

Sketch on the top face of the box. Added four horizontal lines, two coming in from each side, constrained equal length with the two bottom ones collinear and the two top ones collinear. Dimensioned one line with WindowHeight, set the spacing between the pairs with WindowLength, and set WindowOffset from the open entry side to the bottom line. Added two semicircles with centers vertically collinear with the inner points of the lines, then closed the sketch with a line collinear with the outside of the box. Exited the sketch and used extrude cut, through all, to cut the catch windows.

Sketch on the opposite end from where the buckle enters, for the strap feature. Added a horizontal and vertical center line, then a rectangle 8 mm by 33 mm centered on them.

Extruded this sketch 15 mm.

Selected one of the wide faces created by the previous extrude and sketched on it. Selected the whole face, used offset, dimensioned it 5 mm, and reversed it so the offset goes inward. Exited the sketch and used extrude cut, through all, to create the strap slot.

Added 1 mm fillets to all outer corners.

Design Decisions

  • Clearance from L04: The 0.5 mm cavity clearance (per side) and 0.4 mm thickness clearance come directly from L04's finding that 0.5 mm is the minimum clearance needed for FDM parts to fit together reliably.
  • Parametric constraints: All major dimensions driven by global variables, so changing b, h, L, or gap automatically updates both prongs and the cavity.
  • T-shaped center prong: Adds compression strength and ensures the male part seats fully against the female part during insertion.
  • Catch window shape: Parallel lines with semicircle joins allow smooth engagement and easy manual release without sharp corners.
  • Strap slot: Offset inward by 5 mm to create a slot that can hold a webbing band or loop.

3D Printing and Test

Preprocessor: Bambu Studio Settings

Both parts printed together on the Bambu Lab A1 with Overture Matte PLA, 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layer height.

Build orientation: Flat on the bed. The prongs and cavity run along the Y axis, the beam length direction. Bending happens in X, in the layer plane, not across layers. This keeps the strength in the printed lines where it matters.

Walls: 5 at 0.4 mm each, 2 mm solid on the bending faces. Top and bottom: 5 layers each, 1.0 mm. Infill: 20 percent Gyroid. These match the design assumptions from the stress analysis.

Supports: Tree supports, everywhere. The catch windows and internal cavity features needed them, which caused problems later.

Testing and Results

Snap fit: Both prongs snap into the catch windows and hold. The male part stays inserted until squeezed.

Release: Squeezing the prongs together makes them flex inward by about 1.2 mm, as designed, which lifts the lips out of the catch windows. Release is smooth and repeatable.

Print quality: Most surfaces are clean. The support attachment marks on the inside top edges of the cavity are rough and slightly raised, which affects how smoothly the lips slide in and out.

Lessons Learned

Lip size: The 1.0 mm lip height works but feels fragile. A 1.5 mm or 2.0 mm lip would be more robust and easier to handle.

Catch window size: The 2.0 mm window height is tight. A 2.5 to 3.0 mm window would leave more room for fingers to operate the release without pinching.

Support placement: Supports inside the cavity roughed up the catch surfaces and made them harder to clean. Printing the female part cavity-side-up (inverted) would have eliminated internal supports and improved dimensional accuracy. The tradeoff is that the buckle base mounting point would then need support on the bottom.

Overall assessment: The buckle works as designed and holds reliably. With a second iteration using larger lips, bigger catch windows, and an inverted build orientation to avoid internal supports, this would be a solid, practical design.
